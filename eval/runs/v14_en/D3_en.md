**Start by moving the status updates out of the meeting and onto a short written post that everyone reads before they walk in. The reason is that most of the 90 minutes is spent on updates that matter to only one or two people, and reading is much faster than listening.**

### ① Three-line post before the meeting
- What to do: Each person writes three lines on a shared page by Monday noon. The lines are "what I am working on", "what I start next", and "where I need help". The first 10 minutes of the meeting is silent reading. Then the group talks only about lines where two names touch the same thing.
- Why it fits: The meeting stays on the calendar, so your rule is respected. The common answer is "make the meeting shorter or stricter". This one changes what the time is for, not how long it is.
- Where it fails: It fails if people skip the post or write vague lines like "working on the project".
- First experiment: The whole team, for 2 weeks. Keep going if at least 8 of 10 people post on time and the meeting ends 30 minutes early or more.

### ② Topic blocks, leave when your block ends
- What to do: The organizer splits the agenda into 15 to 20 minute blocks, each with a named owner and a list of people who need to be there. The agenda goes out the day before. Everyone else may stay or leave. They can also use the freed time to work.
- Why it fits: The 90 minutes still exist and the meeting still happens. The common answer is "invite fewer people", which risks the very information gaps you have now. Here everyone can still read the notes and join any block they choose.
- Where it fails: It fails if people do not know in advance which block affects them, or if the leaving feels rude.
- First experiment: The organizer, in the next meeting. Keep going if the average person sits through under 45 minutes and nobody reports missing something they needed.

### ③ A one-line "I am starting this" note
- What to do: Before anyone starts a task that will take more than half a day, they post one line in the team chat. It says what they are about to build or fix. Anyone who is doing the same thing, or has done it, replies within an hour.
- Why it fits: It uses the chat tool you already have and costs about 30 seconds per task. The common answer is "share more in the weekly meeting". That is too late, because duplicated work has already started by then. This catches the overlap on day one.
- Where it fails: It fails if people forget to post, or if the team treats a reply as a veto and starts arguing in the thread.
- First experiment: The whole team, for 3 weeks. Keep going if you catch at least 2 overlaps early, or if nobody reports duplicated work.

### Wildcard: Report on your neighbor, not yourself
- What to do: Each person reports what the teammate next to them is doing, using the shared page to prepare. The teammate may correct them only in the last 30 seconds. Then rotate the pairs every week.
- Why it fits: Nobody is bored by listening to their own update. Each person must read and understand one other person's work, so the knowledge spreads. The common answer has each person speak about their own work in turn.
- Where it fails: It is risky. People may feel put on the spot, or get things wrong and spread wrong facts.
- First experiment: Try it with 4 volunteers for one meeting. Keep going if all 4 say they now understand a teammate's work better and the corrections are small.

### Next steps
1. Before changing anything, ask three people how much of the 90 minutes was actually useful to them. This checks whether my guess about the cause is right.
2. If the guess holds, try ① in the next meeting.
3. Add ③ the same week, since it needs no meeting change.

### Reasoning
- Goal: everyone knows what others are doing, without sitting through 90 minutes of talk that does not concern them.
- Constraints: ten people, a weekly meeting that cannot be canceled, and the 90-minute slot exists.
- Cause (a guess): The meeting is used to give every person a turn on status. So each person hears nine updates, most of which do not apply to them. If the guess is wrong, and the boredom comes from long debates, then ② matters more than ①.
- Contradiction: The whole team must be in the room to share information, and at the same time must not be there because it wastes their time. I separate these by condition. Only people who are affected stay for each topic. ① and ② resolve this directly. ① moves the information into writing, and ② lets people leave when it stops concerning them.
- ③ comes from the resource "existing chat tool" and from the nuisance of duplicated work. It catches the problem at the start instead of at the weekly meeting.
- The wildcard came from reversing roles, which means doing the thing the other way around. It does not directly resolve the contradiction.

### Appendix
- Common answers I set aside: shorten the meeting, a strict agenda, a timer for each person, a standing meeting, a recorded video, a shared task board, a weekly email summary, and meeting less often.
- Idea titles from the sweep: split the meeting by topic [segmentation], pull out only the overlap items [extraction], send the update before the meeting [prior action], report on a neighbor [the other way around], a chat note at task start [feedback], silent reading [replace with another channel], rotating hosts [dynamics], a color on the board for tasks that overlap [change of color].
- Terms: "separation by condition" means the answer differs depending on the situation. "Feedback" means using a result to change what happens next.
